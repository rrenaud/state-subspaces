State Subspaces

How to tell whether two groups vary along the same directions, using principal angles between subspaces. The example is the 50 US states, grouped by Census region.

Subspaces, not bases

Take a group of data points and its top r principal components. Those components span a subspace: the directions along which the group varies most. Two groups can be compared by asking how much their subspaces overlap.

A subspace has many bases. Rotate the basis vectors among themselves and they still span the same subspace. So a comparison should depend only on the subspaces, not on which basis vectors happened to come out of the computation. Comparing basis vectors one by one fails this test.

Principal angles

Principal angles answer the question greedily:

  1. Find the unit vector in S₁ and the unit vector in S₂ that have the smallest angle between them. That angle is θ₁, and the two vectors are the first pair of principal vectors.
  2. Inside each subspace, restrict to directions orthogonal to the pair you just found, and repeat. That gives θ₂, then θ₃, up to θ_r.

If θ₁ = 0°, the two subspaces share one direction exactly. If every angle is 0°, they are the same subspace. If every angle is 90°, each direction in one is orthogonal to each direction in the other.

How to compute them. Put orthonormal bases of the two subspaces in the columns of U₁ and U₂. The small r×r matrix U₁ᵀU₂ holds every dot product between a basis vector of S₁ and one of S₂. Its entries depend on the bases chosen. Its singular values do not. The SVD U₁ᵀU₂ = P · diag(cos θᵢ) · Qᵀ finds the rotation of each basis (U₁P and U₂Q) that matches the vectors up one to one, and the singular values are the cosines of the principal angles.

A worked example in three dimensions, with r = 2:

S₁ = span{ x, y } the floor plane S₂ = span{ x, (y+z)/√2 } the floor plane tilted 45° about the x axis U₁ᵀU₂ = [ x·x x·(y+z)/√2 ] = [ 1 0 ] singular values 1, 0.707 [ y·x y·(y+z)/√2 ] [ 0 0.707 ] θ₁ = 0° (both contain x), θ₂ = 45° Use a different basis for S₁: { (x+y)/√2, (x−y)/√2 } U₁ᵀU₂ = [ 0.707 0.5 ] every entry changed, [ 0.707 −0.5 ] singular values still 1, 0.707 overlap = ‖U₁ᵀU₂‖²_F / r = (1² + 0.707²) / 2 = 0.75 (same with either basis)

A one-number summary. The overlap score ‖U₁ᵀU₂‖²_F / r = (cos²θ₁ + … + cos²θ_r) / r is the average of the squared cosines. Pick a unit vector in S₂ at random and project it onto S₁: the score is the squared length it keeps, on average. It is 1 when the subspaces coincide, 0 when they are orthogonal, and the same in either direction.

The floor. A random unit vector in ℝⁿ keeps, on average, 1/n of its squared length along any one fixed direction, so r/n when projected onto any r-dimensional subspace. Two unrelated subspaces therefore score about r/n. In the 8 dimensions of the state data, with r = 2, that is 0.25, which is not small.

The ceiling. Even one group does not produce the same subspace twice: a different sample of its data moves it. Comparing two halves of one group's data estimates how high a score can realistically go. A score means something only relative to both the floor and this ceiling.

Try it on the 50 states

Each state is a point in 8 dimensions: population and density on a log scale, median household income, median age, share with a bachelor's degree, share urban, share 65 and older, and poverty rate, each z-scored across all 50 states. Each Census region is a group, and its subspace is spanned by its top r principal components.

Each basis vector is a unit-length weighted sum of the features, and its squared weights add up to 1. So a weight of 0.7 means about half of that direction (0.7² ≈ 0.49) is that one feature. Features with large weights are drawn bold, and those near zero are faded. The plots show all 50 states projected onto each basis, with each region in its own color and shape. Try Rotate: the basis vectors, the plot and the matrix change, and the angles do not.

The South is the most nationally typical region. It holds the bottom of the affluence scale (the five lowest states are all Southern) and Maryland near the top, so its spread along that direction is 1.2 times the nation's. It also spans the age direction, from young Texas, Georgia and Oklahoma to old West Virginia, Delaware and Florida. Its top two directions are the national ones, at 7° and 19°. Two checks:

  • Containment. The South is part of "All states", which pulls the two together. Compared with the other 34 states instead, the South still scores 0.73.
  • Size. The South sits at the 89th percentile of random 16-state groups. That is high, but about one random group in nine does as well, so "typical" is the right word, not "exceptional".

The Midwest is distinct, but not in its directions. Its overlap with the nation is low, but at the 13th percentile it is not unusual for 12 states. What is unusual is how little it varies along the national directions: a quarter of the nation's spread on affluence and a fifth on age. None of 2,000 random 12-state groups is that compressed on both. Midwestern states are alike in wealth and age, so what is left to vary is size, Ohio and Illinois against the Dakotas, and that becomes the Midwest's top direction.

This is a limit of the overlap score. It measures which directions a group varies along, not how much. A group can have nearly the national directions with much less variance along them. Checking the spread, or the singular values, catches what the angles miss.

The Northeast's third direction is its own. At r = 2 it is unremarkable (20th percentile). At r = 3 its third angle is 83° and its overlap falls to the 2nd percentile. Its top direction is urban against rural, New Jersey and Massachusetts against Maine and Vermont, with the rural states older.

The West is unremarkable. Its top direction ties age to wealth (older states like New Mexico and Montana are poorer and smaller than Utah, California and Colorado), which the nation's directions do not. But it sits at the 37th percentile, so this data cannot tell that pattern from chance.

Two lessons for comparing groups this way. First, judge each group against random groups of the same size, not just against the floor: the floor ignores how noisy a small group's components are. Second, look at variance as well as angles: two groups can share directions and still differ a lot in how much they vary along them.

Where this came from

This page was written to explain how to compare low-rank adapters trained on different domains. Each adapter's update writes into a subspace, and the same tools apply: principal angles, a random floor and a split-half ceiling. With adapters, n is in the thousands and r is 4 to 8, so the floor r/n is close to zero, and the ceiling from a second training seed matters more than the floor.
